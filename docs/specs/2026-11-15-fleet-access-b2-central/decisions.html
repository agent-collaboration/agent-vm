Your four choices

1 · What happens when the reply to “create workspace” is lost?

Background. When you press Create on the admin page, the page sends Central a request with an operation ID, a unique ID the server makes for that one attempt. Central creates the workspace, using the operation ID as the workspace's ID, and keeps its answer in a request ledger under that ID. If the reply is lost on the way back, for example Wi-Fi drops or the tab closes, the page can send the same operation ID again. Central then returns the stored answer instead of creating a second workspace. Central keeps those stored answers for 24 hours (organization_workspace_plans.rs:103–135).

Example. Monday 10:00 you create “billing” and your connection drops before the reply arrives. You don't know if it worked. On Tuesday at 11:00, more than 24 hours later, you press Try again.

What you see on Tuesday

“Already created on your earlier attempt”, with the workspace. Central says “name in use”, so the page looks up the workspace whose ID is your operation ID. If it's there with the same name, it was yours. If another workspace holds the name, you're told the name is taken.

What changes

Nothing on Central. One lookup in the page's flow (M4).

What it costs

The reply after a day is built from that lookup, not Central's byte-for-byte original. You see the same outcome either way.

Picture

What you see on Tuesday

The exact original reply, whenever you retry, even a year later.

What changes

Central stops expiring its request ledger for creates. That changes stored data on Central, so Lead A designs it with you first.

What it costs

The ledger grows without limit, and B2 can't build until Lead A's change lands.

Picture

On 16 Nov you asked “are you being pendantic? is this a areal issue?”. It isn't a real issue: both options give the right outcome and never a duplicate. So this is A, today's Central behaviour plus the lookup. You also asked why there's a ledger at all. Central's request_log (workspace_request_log) is request safety, not workspace data: for 24 hours it replays the same request's answer and refuses a reused ID with different content. After that, the workspace itself, created under the operation ID, is the durable fact the lookup reads.

2 · Changing sign-in's signing key: may sign-in pause for a few minutes?

The key. Sign-in has one signing key pair, kept in Better Auth's jwks table. The private half signs. The public half is published at sign-in's key URL; Central fetches it from there and keeps a copy for up to 5 minutes.

What it signs. Each organization's statement (“organization X exists, set up by this owner”), which Central stores and checks before doing any work for that organization. It also signs people's tokens, which live at most 5 minutes and simply expire.

Who starts a rotation, and when. You do, rarely: on a schedule, or if the key might have leaked. You start it from the hidden owner-only maintenance page on sign-in (decision 4).

The danger. A request already in progress can sign with the old key just after step 1 finished, and land after step 2. That organization is left with a statement Central can't verify, and its Fleet calls fail until someone notices and repairs it.

Example. You rotate the key on a Sunday morning. An owner in another organization is creating a workspace at that moment, and their request renews their organization's statement.

What people see

For roughly 10 to 15 minutes, new sign-ins, organization creation and admin actions answer “Maintenance, try again shortly”. Command lines already signed in keep working until their tokens expire (at most 5 minutes). That owner's workspace creation waits; they retry after the window.

What changes

  • A deployment setting that turns maintenance on.
  • A one-row maintenance_pause table. Every write of an organization statement checks it, so a request that started before the pause can't save its old-key statement afterwards.
  • The steps: pause, drain, re-sign all, withdraw, wait 5 min 10 s, restart Central, check, reopen.

What it costs

Minutes without new sign-ins once in a long while, and one new table (choosing A approves it).

What changes

3 · The engine repair console: in B2, or its own feature later?

Background. Lead A's new Central engine runs background work for each organization: timers, automations, and moving old notices and workspace drains onto the engine. When a job fails for good, the engine retires it rather than retrying forever. Someone then needs to see the retired jobs and either retry or discard each one. Central offers four routes for that: health, list retired, retry, discard. They need a new owner permission, organization:repairWork. B2 adds that permission either way, because Central's engine expects it.

Example. An automation timer in your organization keeps failing because its workspace was deleted. The engine retires it.

What you see now

Nothing new on the page. The retired job stays recorded on Central; nothing is lost. You can't retry or discard it from Switchboard until the console feature ships.

What changes in B2

The permission in the owner role, and its rollout (one revision per organization).

What it costs

Repairs wait for the later feature, “Engine operations: inspect and repair retired work”.

Picture

What you see

An “Engine” section on the admin page: health, the list of retired jobs with their reason, and Retry and Discard buttons. The CLI gets the same commands. You retry or discard the timer yourself.

What changes in B2

Four forwarded routes with typed results (Retry and Discard carry their own operation ID, like create), paging, a guarded page section and CLI commands.

What it costs

B2 grows by a whole feature, which would be designed and reviewed before B2 builds.

Picture

This is sequencing, which you handed to me on 16 Nov: “i dont care about of merging or operations you have to think and propelry seqeuknce things”. B2 adds the permission, and the console comes next as its own feature, with its own design and review.

4 · How do you start a maintenance operation in production?

Background. Two operations need to run inside the sign-in service, because only it holds Better Auth's private keys and its database:

  • the key rotation above;
  • the one-time “permission digest roll” that rolls out the new permission.

Today there's no way to trigger such an operation. The Node host has no command-line tool, and the Cloudflare host only has the gateway's management binding.

Example. You decide to rotate the key. What do you actually do?

What you do

Sign in as the deployment owner, confirm your second factor, open the maintenance page, press “Prepare key rotation”, and read the report. Same on Cloudflare and Node.

What changes

One project Better Auth endpoint and page. Everyone except the configured owner, with a factor confirmed in the last 15 minutes, gets “not found”.

What it costs

A deployment-wide operation sits on the public sign-in site. Someone who stole your session and your second factor could start a rotation. They could already do as much damage as the owner in other ways.

Picture

What you do

On Cloudflare: deploy a tiny temporary Worker that can reach sign-in's private entry, call it with a secret from your vault, then delete it. On Node: log in to the server and run a new sign-in-maintenance command.

What changes

A private Worker entry, a throwaway maintenance Worker config, and a new Node command.

What it costs

Two different procedures, and a deploy-then-delete step each time. Nothing on the public site.
